Jerry Marzinsky and his interpretation of distressing voices

A psychotherapist's spiritual explanation of voices, the experiences behind it, and the distinction between testimony, treatment claims and Qur'anic teaching.

Introduction

Belief in the unseen invites us to take spiritual life seriously. It also calls for care when we explain another person's suffering. This episode introduces Jerry Marzinsky, a retired psychotherapist who interprets some distressing voices as the activity of hostile spiritual beings. Ahmad Dadoosh sees similarities between this account and Islamic teachings about devils, while saying that his episode is an introduction, not a complete demonstration or a recommendation of Marzinsky's treatment method.[1]

The distinction matters throughout the discussion. The Qur'an teaches us about Satan; an interview describes what one practitioner believes happened to particular people. Understanding his testimony does not require treating every explanation or claimed result as established knowledge.

Who is Marzinsky?

Marzinsky's published biography describes more than thirty-five years of work across psychiatric institutions, prisons, mental-health services and emergency departments. His career began at Central State Hospital in Georgia. He lists a psychology degree from Temple University, a master's degree in rehabilitation counselling from the University of Georgia and two years of doctoral study. These are his own biographical statements; they do not identify him as a psychiatrist or as someone who completed a doctorate.[2]

His central claim grew out of conversations with people who heard voices. He came to believe that the apparent personalities, intentions and recurring patterns of these voices pointed to external agents. His website describes them as parasitic beings that cultivate distress and feed upon it. This establishes what he argues, rather than independently proving the proposed mechanism.[3]

What are the disorders?

The episode moves between psychosis, schizophrenia, intrusive thoughts and distress after trauma. These terms should not be collapsed into one condition. Psychosis can involve hearing or seeing things others do not, alongside changes in beliefs, thought and behaviour. It has several possible causes and does not always mean schizophrenia. NIMH identifies genetic and developmental factors, stress or trauma, sleep deprivation, some medicines and substance misuse among relevant considerations.[4]

Dadoosh interprets vulnerability after trauma or drug exposure through a spiritual framework. He acknowledges differences between people: exposure does not lead everyone to the same outcome. His further suggestion that an unseen being exploits that vulnerability remains his interpretation. A survivor's suffering is not grounds for blaming them or making a judgment about their faith.[5]

One formative story concerns a woman whose wishes allegedly differed from those expressed by her voices. Marzinsky reportedly found that he could question the voices through her replies. For him, the apparent dialogue suggested another intelligence. The story preserves his reason for changing his mind, but a conversation of this kind does not independently identify its cause.[6]

The voices heard by the patient

A proposed pattern

In Dadoosh's account, the voices disguise themselves as a person's own thoughts, undermine confidence and sometimes discourage relationships or religious practice. He connects this with the Islamic concern about deceptive whispering. The more detailed claims about hierarchies of entities and their replacement when discovered belong to the episode's proposed model; they are not established by a reported conversation alone.[7]

The Psalm 23 account

Marzinsky describes giving patients Psalm 23 and asking them to report what happened when they read it while hearing voices. In the linked interview, he says the participating patients consistently reported hostile reactions from their voices. The inspected passage gives no sample size, comparison group or independently assessed outcomes. It is an informal exercise reported by its practitioner, rather than a controlled experiment.[8]

The prayer is Psalm 23, whose opening presents the Lord as a shepherd and whose later verses express trust amid danger. Identifying the passage resolves an uncertain Arabic caption reading; it does not verify a treatment effect.[9]

Dadoosh makes a separate religious point: he is not asking Muslims to replace Qur'anic recitation with the Psalms. His interest is in the reported reaction to prayer, which he interprets as consistent with hostility to remembrance of God.[10]

The discussion also links frightening entertainment, anxiety and exhaustion to Marzinsky's idea of beings feeding on distress. The episode offers anecdotes for that interpretation, without a measurement of the proposed energy or a study establishing the direction of cause and effect.[11]

Medication

The episode's own examples acknowledge that medication can help. A hairdresser reportedly worked and functioned better while taking it, but returned to hospital after voices discouraged her from continuing. The voices' accusation that a doctor was poisoning her is part of the reported experience, not a reliable description of the medicine.[12]

Marzinsky also reportedly asked a group of fifteen volunteers to tell him what their voices were saying during conversations. Dadoosh presents this as a way of noticing patterns and resistance. Without a published protocol, comparison group and assessed outcomes, the account cannot establish how well a treatment works.[13]

The broader assertion that people never recover with psychiatric care needs correction. WHO states that at least a third of people with schizophrenia achieve complete remission of symptoms. It also describes care involving medication, psychological work, family support and rehabilitation. Recovery varies between people; neither permanent hopelessness nor a guaranteed cure is justified.[14]

NIMH similarly describes treatment as more than medication alone. It includes therapy, family involvement and support for work or study. Medication benefits and side effects should be discussed with a qualified clinician. An interview about spiritual causes is not a basis for independently stopping treatment.[4]

More than voices

The next section broadens the testimony to visions, bodily experiences and apparent knowledge of hidden events. Dadoosh retells a private journal about an out-of-body encounter, a woman's vision of creatures near a clinician, and an alleged former burglar's account of receiving warnings from voices. These remain reports passed through the practitioner and presenter; the underlying diary, clinical records and independent witnesses have not been checked.[15][16][17]

Another story involves a voice objecting to Marzinsky's interference in its way of life. His own website recounts that incident. The striking feature is the apparent separation between patient and speaker, which he interprets spiritually. Its presence on his website verifies the attribution of the story, not the identity of a second being.[18]

The episode also mentions a passage from The Voice of Knowledge, attributed to don Miguel Ruiz, and a loud sound reportedly heard after it was read. The exact book passage and any independent record of the sound remain unchecked. They therefore cannot carry the weight of a demonstrated physical event caused by a spirit.[19][20]

The recollection of electroconvulsive treatment

Marzinsky's disturbing account of a treatment he reportedly witnessed in the 1970s belongs to this section too. The presenter interprets temporary relief in spiritual terms. No contemporaneous record of that particular procedure has been inspected.[21]

Modern electroconvulsive therapy should be described separately: NIMH explains that it uses general anaesthesia and a muscle relaxant, and discusses both its clinical uses and possible memory problems. A historical recollection cannot substitute for an accurate description of current practice or an individual medical assessment.[22]

Difficulties after his discovery

Dadoosh describes professional opposition, instructions to stop questioning voices and colleagues who allegedly offered support in private. Those experiences help explain Marzinsky's distrust of the institutions in which he worked. They remain his side of a workplace history; employer records and responses have not been examined.[23][24]

The episode develops a wider criticism of a framework that excludes spiritual explanations. It includes a hypothetical example of research into prayer for rain. That example is a thought experiment, not a rejected study whose data can be examined.[25]

Concern about institutional assumptions can be discussed without claiming that research into psychosis is absent. NIMH describes clinical trials and its RAISE programme investigating care after an initial episode. The validity of a particular spiritual explanation still depends on the evidence offered for it.[4]

Resignation and the Mace Energy Method

Two dates need separating. Marzinsky's biography says he retired as a psychiatric evaluator in 2015 and became a certified MACE practitioner in 2023. The latter date should not be presented as the established date of his retirement or licence surrender.[26]

The Mace Energy Method, associated with John Mace and the philosophy called Causism, describes exercises addressing negative emotional patterns. The provider's own FAQ says the method is not a cure, even while promoting benefits. Its description identifies the approach; it is not independent clinical evidence for it.[27]

In the original interview, Marzinsky estimates a 15–20% recovery rate among people his group worked with and describes trauma relief as almost 100%, then qualifies that as at least in the nineties. He also claims that a major trauma can be addressed in an hour. The inspected passage supplies no sample size, agreed definition of recovery, comparison group or follow-up data. These are practitioner claims, not reliable promises to a reader.[28]

Dadoosh also discusses payment for spiritual care. The hadith about al-Fatihah and a flock of sheep provides a basis for the permissibility of payment in that reported case. It does not establish the competence, honesty or success of every modern practitioner who charges a fee.[29]

Near the end of this section, Dadoosh explicitly says that he is not denying useful medication. He proposes considering medical, behavioural and spiritual care together. That qualification should remain visible alongside his criticism of psychiatry; the episode does not supply a tested treatment protocol for doing so.[30]

The Qur'anic perspective

The closing section returns to revelation. Satan seeks to frighten, divide and distract people from Allah. Qur'an 3:175 directs believers to fear Allah rather than Satan's followers. Qur'an 5:91 describes hostility and neglect of remembrance and prayer in the specific context of intoxicants and gambling.[31][32]

Qur'an 58:10 connects harmful secret counsel with grief, then immediately places harm under Allah's permission and calls believers to trust Him. This final part is essential: our attention should not become trapped in fear of unseen enemies.[33]

The episode also recalls Qur'an 7:201: those mindful of Allah remember Him when troubled by Satan and regain clarity. Remembrance and seeking Allah's protection belong at the heart of the believer's response.[34]

These teachings provide a spiritual foundation. They do not identify the cause of every voice, certify an energy theory or guarantee that a medical condition will disappear after a particular exercise. We can take the unseen seriously, examine testimony carefully and respond to a suffering person with compassion and appropriate care.
