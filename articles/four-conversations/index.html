Four conversations on jinn and altered consciousness

A medium's purported messages, a soldier's reported ordeal, Graham Hancock's spiritual search, and a convert's experience with psychedelics.

Introduction

Jinn are real creatures of Allah, but a strange experience is not automatically an encounter with them. This episode brings four Western conversations into one discussion: a preacher questioning purported spirits, a soldier's reported ordeal, a writer's search for ancient knowledge and a convert's account of altered perception. They invite discernment about testimony, revelation and what an interview can actually establish.[1]

The conversations are connected by a question: when someone reports contact with an unseen intelligence, who should be trusted? Islam offers an answer rooted in Allah's revelation rather than in the authority claimed by an unseen speaker. That conviction need not turn every disturbing event, drug experience or medical symptom into proof of possession.

A British preacher questions purported jinn through a medium

Ahmad Dadoosh first discusses a conversation posted by British Muslim preacher Imran Hussein. A woman relays messages attributed to a group calling itself the “Nine.” Hussein presses the speakers about God and their identity. The medium's role matters: the audience hears a human intermediary report messages, rather than observing the purported beings independently.[2][3]

Their claims about a God who learns, changes or creates because of loneliness conflict with the Islamic understanding of Allah's complete knowledge and perfection. Hussein replies from that understanding. The Qur'an describes Allah as fully aware of plans made against His messenger; this is a theological standard for judging the message, not a laboratory identification of the speakers.[4][5]

As the discussion becomes confrontational, Hussein takes the language of threats and repeated lives as further reason to distrust the speakers. Dadoosh suggests possible jinn deception, but their self-description as an ancient council, the number of entities involved and any particular identity remain unverified. Even the medium expresses uncertainty about trusting what she relays. The useful lesson is therefore narrower than “the Nine were proved to be jinn”: an unseen claimant does not become a religious authority by sounding ancient or powerful.[6]

Dadoosh then recalls the magicians who turned toward Allah in the account of Moses. In Qur'an 20:73, they tell Pharaoh that he had compelled them to practise magic. Their later inner motives, and the comparison between their situation and this medium's, are the presenter's reflections rather than details established by the verse. The episode does not establish that the medium later accepted Islam.[7][8]

The Canadian soldier account

The next conversation comes from Joe Rogan's interview with former Canadian soldier Devon Larratt. Larratt recounts disturbing behaviour attributed to another soldier during a deployment in Iraq: unfamiliar speech, claimed knowledge of private matters, consultation with a chaplain and a return to Canada. Dadoosh reads this through the possibility of possession.[9]

The original interview makes one distinction especially important. Larratt says he was not on the tour where the incident occurred. His account is therefore secondhand, although he says he knew the man and others involved. He speaks uncertainly about the duration, and says the soldier remained troubled afterward; the original does not establish a complete cure. Larratt refers to “Urbil,” commonly written Erbil, while the episode speculates about another Iraqi place-name. No clinical record, chaplain's report or direct interview with the soldier has been inspected.[10]

A sincere witness can accurately recount what he heard while the underlying event remains difficult to evaluate. Speech that sounds unfamiliar, a claim to know private information and a later improvement each require their own evidence. The story deserves a faithful description; it cannot serve as a diagnosis for an absent person.[11]

Graham Hancock and drugs of consciousness

Graham Hancock's conversation with The Diary of a CEO moves from a proposed lost ancient civilization to shamanism and altered states. His book Fingerprints of the Gods presents his case for an unidentified civilization of remote antiquity. He discusses culture-bearing sages and suggests that visionary practices might reveal a wider realm. These are Hancock's theories and interpretations, not findings established by the interview.[12][13]

Archaeologists have publicly challenged Ancient Apocalypse, the series associated with Hancock's ancient-civilization thesis. The Society for American Archaeology objects to its treatment of archaeology and evidential claims. Dadoosh's further suggestion that an entertainment platform seeks to normalize occult belief is his interpretation of its motives, not something the series listing itself demonstrates.[14][15]

Hancock also discusses ayahuasca and the possibility of experiences outside ordinary consciousness. He contrasts mediated religion with direct spiritual experience and says he believes a wider realm exists. Dadoosh warns that a striking vision can become an unreliable guide, particularly when its source is unknown. Islamic revelation offers a criterion other than the intensity of an experience.[16]

The episode alludes to the removal of Hancock's 2013 TEDx talk. TED's own explanation is more specific: editors moved the talk to their blog with critical discussion about unsupported claims. They did not thereby establish that every reported vision was false, nor did relocation itself prove a suppression campaign.[17]

Islamic caution about divinatory knowledge appears in a report in Sunan Abi Dawud connecting astrology with a branch of magic. It does not mean that every discussion of ancient astronomy, every altered state and every illness has the same religious ruling or cause.[18]

A European convert recounts an altered state

In the final interview, Bobby Ristov describes growing up in Germany in a family from North Macedonia. During a period of severe distress, he took psychedelic mushrooms and later reported a panoramic perception of a tree, unfamiliar visual patterns and a loss of his ordinary sense of self. Dadoosh describes this as a possible experience of the soul outside the body. Ristov's own testimony establishes what he remembers perceiving; it does not establish that his soul literally left his body.[19][20]

Ristov describes further spiritual searching, including shamanic practices, and dissatisfaction with what they gave him. He says he began reading the Qur'an intending to defend Christianity against Islam; instead, its monotheism resonated with him. Later in the interview he speaks explicitly of taking the shahadah, the declaration of faith. The sequence is his own account, and it should not be made into a general rule about everyone who uses psychedelics.[21][22]

Such substances can alter perception and carry medical risks; those effects alone cannot identify the cause or religious meaning of a particular image. The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health lists anxiety, paranoia and other adverse effects among possible risks of psilocybin. That clinical context supports caution without proving or disproving the existence of jinn.[23]

Across these four conversations, the strongest conclusion is a disciplined one. Revelation grounds belief in jinn; witnesses tell us what they experienced or heard; independent records help test particular historical and clinical claims. Faith does not require us to treat every ambiguous report as settled proof, and care for a distressed person begins with honesty about what we do and do not know.
