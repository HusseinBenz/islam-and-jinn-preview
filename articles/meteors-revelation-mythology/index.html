Meteors between astronomy, revelation, and mythology

Shooting stars, the guarding of heaven, the Black Stone, and the meanings people have attached to stones from the sky.

Introduction

A streak of light across the night sky can inspire wonder, fear or a search for meaning. Islam directs that wonder towards Allah, who created the heavens. It also teaches us to distinguish what revelation tells us from meanings that people attach to an event without evidence.

The ninth episode of al-Sabeel’s series explores that distinction through astronomy and a review of Fireball: Visitors From Darker Worlds, the 2020 documentary by Werner Herzog and Clive Oppenheimer. Ahmad Dadoosh follows its journey through landscapes shaped by impacts and communities whose traditions give those places religious meaning.[1]

He interprets many of these traditions as examples of deception by devils. That is the episode’s theological interpretation. The presence of a tradition, its historical development and the unseen cause proposed for it remain separate questions.[2]

Comets, meteoroids, meteors and meteorites

These words describe different objects or stages of an event. Keeping them distinct prevents confusion when moving between astronomy and religious language.[3]

Term Meaning
Comet A body containing ice and rocky material, such as Halley, moving around the Sun.
Meteoroid A small rocky body still in space.
Meteor The luminous atmospheric phenomenon produced when a meteoroid enters at high speed.
Meteorite Material that survives the passage and reaches the ground.

Halley’s comet has an average orbital period of about 76 years. The Arabic captions give an apparent eight-year reading; the astronomical figure is corrected here, while the recording still needs checking to determine whether this was a caption error or a spoken slip.[4]

Meteor showers can recur as Earth passes through a trail of debris. NASA estimates that roughly 44 metric tonnes of meteoritic material falls on Earth each day. Most incoming material does not arrive as a large stone; spectacular impacts are a very different scale of event.[5]

Meteors and the guarding of heaven in Islam

The Qur’an describes the lowest heaven as adorned and guarded against rebellious devils. In Surah al-Saffat, one who snatches a hearing is pursued by a piercing flame. Surah al-Hijr gives a related description, while Surah al-Jinn records the jinn finding the heaven guarded and a flame awaiting anyone who tries to listen.[6][7][8]

We accept this account of the unseen without pretending that observation supplies every detail of its operation. The episode itself makes a useful qualification: not every meteor we observe must be identified as a strike against a devil. A photograph or a predicted meteor shower does not reveal the particular unseen purpose of every light in the sky.[9]

The Prophet ﷺ also corrected the use of such events as personal omens. In a report preserved by Muslim, his companions described the pre-Islamic belief that a bright meteor signalled the birth or death of a great person. He rejected that explanation and described the transmission of heavenly news, attempts to overhear it, and the addition of falsehood to what was obtained.[10]

The lesson joins belief with restraint. Revelation tells us about the guarding of heaven; it does not authorise us to turn a light in the sky into a prediction about someone’s birth, death or political success.

The Kaaba and the Black Stone

What the texts establish

Dadoosh objects to the documentary’s treatment of the Black Stone. In his account of its opening, a story involving Adam and Eve is combined with a confident meteorite identification. The film’s exact wording has not yet been independently checked, so that criticism is reported here as his reading of the scene.[11]

The Islamic sources should be considered on their own terms. Qur’an 3:96 identifies the House at Bakkah as the first House established for humanity. In al-Bukhari’s report, Abu Dharr asks which mosque was established first; the Prophet ﷺ names al-Masjid al-Haram, followed by al-Masjid al-Aqsa.[12][13]

The Black Stone’s origin in Paradise is reported in Jami al-Tirmidhi 877, which also describes its original whiteness and later darkening through human sins. Al-Tirmidhi’s Arabic assessment is hasan sahih; the displayed Darussalam grading is hasan. These are identifiable textual claims, rather than a geological analysis of a specimen.[14]

Related reports require separate attention. The account describing the Corner and the Maqam as jewels of Paradise appears in al-Tirmidhi 878, but the edition checked displays a weak grade and includes a note about a transmission stopping at the companion. The episode’s additional grading claims should not be collapsed into a single statement that every version is equally authenticated.[15]

A geological proposal is not a confirmed identification

An important scientific reference can be identified precisely. Elsebeth Thomsen’s 1980 paper proposes that the Black Stone might be impactite glass associated with the Wabar craters. Its abstract discusses a possibility; it does not report a definitive identification of the Black Stone as a meteorite.[16]

The claim of origin in Paradise and the proposed material classification concern different questions. Dadoosh’s objection is that a speculative classification should not be allowed to replace revelation. A careful account should likewise avoid suggesting that an unperformed laboratory analysis has settled the material question in either direction.[17]

Impact craters and traditions about spirits

Kandimalal: more than one account

At Wolfe Creek in Western Australia, the episode turns to Indigenous accounts of the crater. Research reported by the University of Portsmouth gives a most likely age of approximately 120,000 years, using two dating methods. The episode’s approximately 150,000-year figure should not be treated as the settled result.[18]

The place is known as Kandimalal in Jaru. The park authority records several traditions, including a rainbow snake emerging from the ground and a separate account of a star falling from the sky. These should not be reduced to one account held by every local person. Nor does recognition by outsiders amount to the first human knowledge of a place already known to its traditional owners.[19]

Dadoosh relates the film’s artist interview to his wider account of spirits presenting themselves as ancestors. The cultural record establishes that such traditions are told; identifying their unseen cause remains his interpretation.[20]

Ensisheim: a stone made into a political omen

The Ensisheim meteorite fell on 7 November 1492. The Natural History Museum Vienna describes how Sebastian Brant used pamphlets to present it as a divine sign favouring Maximilian’s campaign against France. On 26 November, Maximilian ordered the stone preserved in the parish church.[21]

This is a concrete example of a natural event being given political meaning. It also makes the prophetic rejection of birth-and-death omens especially relevant: the physical reality of an event does not establish the claims made in its name.[10]

The Shiva temple in India

The documentary review next visits the Ramgarh Bhand Devra temple. Rajasthan’s tourism department identifies it as a Shiva temple traditionally dated to the tenth century. That supports the place and broad historical identification; it does not by itself establish why its builders selected the site.[22]

Dadoosh discusses the setting through the religious imagery of destruction and renewal associated in the film with Shiva and Parvati. He interprets devotion to these deities within his account of spiritual deception. The original interview with the scientist and the specific explanation of the temple’s founding remain to be checked.[23]

The further suggestion that impact-delivered organic molecules explain a renewed cycle of life also needs a named study. Organic material, living organisms and the emergence of life are distinct claims; the episode does not supply an inspected experiment connecting all three at this site.[24]

Chicxulub and the Maya world

An impact and a later landscape

Chicxulub is an impact structure roughly 180 kilometres across, associated with the catastrophic event approximately 66 million years ago. These are kilometre-scale dimensions: the metre readings in the Arabic captions are not used here.[25]

Its later landscape also matters. The crater became buried beneath limestone, and an arc of sinkholes, or cenotes, marks part of its structure. Water dissolving limestone produced caves and sinkholes that provided freshwater for ancient inhabitants. Individual cenotes should not be described as though each were a separate hole directly punched out by the impact.[26]

Religious meaning and the limits of inference

The episode describes the Maya association of caves with an underworld, ritual activity, and human remains. Dadoosh himself leaves open whether particular remains shown represent sacrifices or burials. Without the original excavation record, that uncertainty should remain intact.[27]

He also interprets Maya astronomical knowledge as connected with spiritual instruction. The episode supplies no independently checked evidence that their calendrical achievements required such instruction; the interpretation should not erase human observation, learning and craftsmanship.[28] The Qur’an teaches that every community has had a warner. This gives a theological basis for discussing guidance beyond the peoples named in scripture, but it does not identify a particular Maya teacher, date or surviving tradition.[29]

The discussion then moves to commemorations of the dead and offerings of food. Dates and communities need care here. A UNESCO account of Huastec celebrations describes practices extending over several days around the turn of October and November. This is added comparative context, not a description of every Maya community or proof of one unchanged festival stretching back thousands of years.[30]

Dadoosh connects reported ideas of spiritual nourishment with the hadith concerning the jinn’s provision. Muslim 450a describes bones over which Allah’s name has been mentioned becoming food for the jinn. That report does not establish that food offered at an unrelated ritual loses its ordinary nutritional value, or identify the recipient of each offering.[31][32]

Accounts of spirits on Mer

The film review returns to Australia through Mer, or Murray Island, in the eastern Torres Strait. Dadoosh recounts an elder’s account of meteors, ancestors and appearances of spirits, and interprets these through his understanding of jinn.[33]

A separate study provides a more specific cultural reference. Carla B. Guedes, Duane W. Hamacher and five coauthors draw on historical material and interviews with Meriam elders to examine Maier, bright meteors, in death customs and mortuary practices. Its abstract supports this local association. It does not verify the film’s every reported detail or establish a supernatural cause.[34]

Recognising a tradition accurately and assessing its religious claims are both necessary. Neither is helped by combining distinct peoples into a single undifferentiated mythology.

Christian reflection, impact risks and the Islamic perspective

Dadoosh appreciates the documentary’s discussion with the Vatican astronomer Guy Consolmagno, finding value in its sense of wonder at creation. A published conversation with the filmmakers independently confirms Consolmagno’s participation and their interest in the relationship between scientific discovery and awe. Consolmagno is a Jesuit lay brother; the term “priest” should not be assumed from that identity.[35][36]

The episode then considers dramatic impact scenarios and the Tunguska event of 1908. NASA’s research account describes modelling that supports an atmospheric explosion of a stony body. Remaining uncertainty about its properties does not provide positive evidence for an exploded spacecraft.[37]

Finally, Dadoosh distinguishes regional disasters from the extinction of humanity and invokes the Islamic account of events before the Hour. His interpretation should not become a dated impact forecast or a reason to dismiss useful observation of hazards. The precise scriptural sequence discussed in this closing passage still needs separate review.[38]

The episode ends with a request for Islamic sources to receive a fair hearing. We need not be embarrassed by what revelation teaches about the unseen. The same confidence should make us careful with translations, honest about uncertainty and willing to correct a historical or scientific detail when the evidence requires it.[39]
